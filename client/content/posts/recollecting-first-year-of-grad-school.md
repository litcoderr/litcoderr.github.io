I’m the kind of person who feels joy when something I imagined turns into something real.

I first learned to code in middle school because I wanted to build my own smartphone app. In my first year of high school, I started studying artificial intelligence, linear algebra, and calculus for a similar reason. Watching AlphaGo, I wanted to understand what was happening inside the model. It felt like the birth of something close to general intelligence, something I had only imagined until then.

That curiosity carried me into college. I interned at AI companies, got my hands dirty with real ml products, and built models I had once only pictured in my head. Releasing them as open source and seeing them used by others was genuinely satisfying.

But computers weren’t the only way I satisfied my urge to create. Making music, writing musicals, singing and acting on stage as an actor and building a live performance from scratch; creation itself was fun, regardless of the medium. Looking back, I think I fully enjoyed that freedom during my undergraduate years.

Then I entered graduate school.

To be honest, it wasn’t a decision I felt strongly about. The world was full of things I wanted to explore, and I knew, perhaps better than most, that pursuing a degree meant giving many of them up and committing to a single, long-term goal. Going into industry, building practical products, and shipping them to the market was an equally attractive path.

Still, whether out of inertia or habit, I chose grad school.

It turned out to be a far more constrained environment than I had expected.

I thought I’d be able to pursue my own research within a lab aligned with my interests. In reality, the structure was different: you join ongoing projects and conduct research within predefined boundaries. I was no exception. Somehow, I ended up taking responsibility for a project that had already begun and had to be done asap.

And then came the wandering.

The project had little to do with what I actually wanted to study. I kept asking myself: How is this supposed to become an actually meaningful paper? Doubt and resistance piled up. Working on something that went against my intuition slowly drained my motivation for research. I kept wondering when I’d finally get to do my work that I had interrest in.

A Breakthrough.

Projects come with requirements—specific components, specific tasks, specific deliverables. Over the year, I realized that if I followed those requirements too literally, no paper would ever come out. The project itself had been planned years ago, while the AI field evolves month by month. There was no way it could keep up.

So I reframed the problem. Instead of starting from the project constraints, I first identified what the actual problem was at the current moment. Then I built a clear argument for why the required components were still useful—if applied in the right way. The logic was surprisingly straightforward. We ran experiments, submitted to a conference, and wrapped up the project.

2025 was an year about learning how to execute a project.
Now, it’s time to do my own research.

Thankfully, my next project aligns much more closely with what interrest me. Even for the parts I’m not fully convinced by, I now have a sense of how to deal with them.

I used to think the lab was a place where doing my own research was nearly impossible. But now that I know I can do it—and will do it, regardless of other opinions—the enjoyable part and the adrenaline rush is comming back,.. finally.

I have a vision in mind and I’m ready to move toward it. I’m determined to build technology that is truly mine—and to carry it all the way into industry as something practical and real. Fingers crossed.

TL;DR — What I Learned from Research Life in 2025

- Do lab projects in a way that makes sense, but always shape them into papers that align with current trends.

- Accept that a paper will never be perfect. Focus on clearly delivering your core claim, and publish it—even if it feels incomplete.

- The best ideas are often the ones no one thought of, yet are obviously important in hindsight. Simple, intuitive solutions win. Sharp intuition matters more than anything.
